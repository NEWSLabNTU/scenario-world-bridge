# Build the road once, then fix each corner

To make the Isaac Sim 6.1 ego respect rocks, potholes, washboard, ruts, crown, berms and ditches, the road has to be real collision geometry and the car's corners have to survive hitting it. Both are within reach without leaving the PhysX 5 vehicle. Generate one height function per road corridor: an ISO 8608 spectrum with gravel's measured spectral break, plus parametric overlays for each feature. Sample it into 5 cm PhysX heightfield tiles for the wheels, into the render mesh for cameras and lidar, and into the source of the localization map, so nothing disagrees about where a bump is. Then repair what the vehicle model lacks. The PhysX corner is a massless spring-damper placed on the ground by one scene query. It has **no wheel mass in the vertical direction, no tire spring, a single linear damper and a rigid stop at full compression**. Isaac 6.1's USD schema exposes **no anti-roll bar, no bump stop and no suspension-limit settings** (checked in the Isaac 6.1 install). Today a 10 cm rock is therefore a step the ray finds late. It produces a damper spike of tens of kN and then a rigid one-step impulse, and the chassis sits about 0.42 m up, where no rock can reach it. The cheapest fixes are a sweep query, the expansion-velocity limit, and a corner-force layer that adds a progressive bump stop, a digressive asymmetric damper and anti-roll bars. They come before rocks as bodies, and long before any multibody rewrite. Expect believable, not predictive, behaviour. The open literature gives the shape of the right answers (a cleat response, a pothole trace, comfort bands) but few force numbers. True wheel hop at 10-15 Hz, which washboard grip loss depends on, is the one effect that needs a deeper change.

This report builds on two earlier ones: `reports/Localized deformable terrain for rally.md` (soil zones, the surface-state raster) and `reports/Real time tire soil lateral dynamics.md` (the per-surface tire curves T1, the brush soil-tire model T2, ESC). Everything here sits on top of that rigid-plus-zone layering and does not replace it. Rocks and roughness change the *geometry* the wheel meets. The surface layer still decides its *friction*, and a soil zone still lowers its plane.

## A rock reaches today's wheel late, as a step, through one ray

The PhysX vehicle finds the ground with exactly two possible queries, set once per vehicle by `physxVehicle:suspensionLineQueryType`: a raycast (the default) or a sweep. The schema's own description says "raycasts are faster while sweeps can represent the wheel shape better and thus react earlier to ground surface changes" (checked in the Isaac 6.1 install; also [omni_usd_schema_physics PhysxVehicleAPI](https://docs.omniverse.nvidia.com/kit/docs/omni_usd_schema_physics/104.0/physx_vehicle_a_p_i_8h_source.html)). The ray runs from the top of the wheel at maximum compression down to maximum droop. The sweep moves a convex cylinder of the wheel's radius and width along the same path, which "generally produces better results but at increased computational cost" ([PhysX 5.5.1 Vehicles guide](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.5.1/docs/Vehicles.html)). Either way the result is **one road plane and one friction value per wheel**. The SDK lets a user component write that plane instead of a query (`eNONE`), but the USD schema offers only raycast and sweep ([PhysX 5.5.1 API](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.5.1/_api_build/structPxVehiclePhysXRoadGeometryQueryType.html)). The aib ego uses the raycast, puts the wheels' queries in a `groundQuery` collision group, and gives the wheels no collision shapes of their own (aib source).

What a single ray does to a rock follows from geometry. A real tire of radius R first touches a step of height h a distance √(2Rh − h²) ahead of the hub. For the spec's 0.370 m wheel and a 10 cm rock that is **about 0.25 m, or 25 ms at 10 m/s** (own arithmetic). The tire then wraps around the edge, and the force rises over that distance and tilts backwards, which is the braking kick a driver feels. The ray registers nothing until the hub is over the rock, and then the full height appears within one or two physics steps: at 180 Hz the car moves 5.6 cm per step at 10 m/s. NVIDIA names the consequence. With a sudden change in road geometry "the wheel may only be placed on the ground by exceeding the maximum compression", and by default the jounce is reset so the wheel sits on the ground, which "can result in large changes to jounce within a single simulation frame" and "large damping forces" (schema doc for `limitSuspensionExpansionVelocity`, checked in the Isaac 6.1 install; [PhysX 5.5.1 Vehicles guide](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.5.1/docs/Vehicles.html)). Run the spec's numbers through aib's tuning (1.6 Hz, damping ratio 0.45, about 440 kg sprung per corner) and they give k ≈ 44 kN/m and c ≈ 4.0 kN·s/m. A 5 cm jounce step taken in one 180 Hz step is a 9 m/s shaft speed, or **about 36 kN of damper force**. If the jump lands inside one of the eight vehicle substeps, it is several times that. A real damper blows off at a few kN (own arithmetic from aib's `params.py`). That spike is the "pop", and it is what would make a car on rocks jump for the wrong reason.

The sweep fixes the timing: the cylinder's front edge meets the rock ahead of the hub. But it is a *rigid* cylinder. The plane snaps from flat to the tangent of the edge, giving a steep slope, a strong longitudinal component and no tire compliance to absorb it. A sweep that starts already overlapping a rock beside the wheel returns an initial-overlap hit. Practitioners filter those out, "not a perfect solution", and also report sweeps falling through complex geometry and batched-query memory running out with several vehicles (UE4/PhysX 4 era; [Unreal forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/implementing-vehicle-wheel-sweeps-for-off-road-game/135211/6); [NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/physx-vehicle-whith-sweep-contact-in-3-4-build/47533)). NVIDIA publishes no per-wheel cost. The vehicle makes 4 queries × 180 per second, so even several times the cost of a ray is negligible next to rendering. Cost is not a reason to stay on raycast, but the number should still be measured.

Games and tyre science go further, and their ladder shows what each step buys. Kunos replaced ACC's single contact point with **five points (two at the front edge, one in the middle, two at the back)** because one point produced a "rail kerb" effect and sudden steering jolts ([OverTake: ACC 5-point tyre blog](https://www.overtake.gg/threads/assetto-corsa-competizione-the-5-point-tyre-model-blog.171148/); [GTPlanet](https://gtplanet.net/assetto-corsa-competizione-update-1-0-7)). Chrono offers a four-point mode and an area-equivalent disc envelope with about 103 height lookups per wheel ([Chrono ChTire.cpp](https://github.com/projectchrono/chrono/blob/main/src/chrono_vehicle/wheeled_vehicle/ChTire.cpp); [Chrono tire models](https://api.projectchrono.org/wheeled_tire.html)). The established answer is the Zegelaar-Schmeitz tandem elliptical cam behind MF-Swift. It turns any profile into an effective height, forward slope, camber slope and rolling-radius variation, and is valid up to 60-100 Hz ([Schmeitz et al., MF-Swift](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/structure-and-parameterization-of-mf-swift-a-magic-formula-based-rigid-ring-tire-model/811835525060100096-17623); [TU/e](https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/a-semi-empirical-three-dimensional-tyre-model-for-rolling-over-ar/)). Schmeitz found **5 cams along and 6 across** the patch the best accuracy-to-cost ratio for a passenger tire ([Stallmann et al. 2014](https://repository.up.ac.za/bitstream/2263/41811/1/Stallmann_Parameterization_2014.pdf)). A numpy version for 32 wheels × 5 cams measured **122 µs per evaluation** on this host, about 2 % of a 180 Hz step (own benchmark, illustrative cam parameters). The same validation study warns where cams fail. On Belgian paving the enveloping model gave "unacceptable results", on corrugation only FTire predicted normal forces, and one-point contact "should be avoided for all ride simulations" ([Stallmann et al. 2014](https://repository.up.ac.za/bitstream/2263/41811/1/Stallmann_Parameterization_2014.pdf)). Full ring-and-node tires (FTire at about one core per tire and 4 kHz, BeamNG's node-beam wheels at 2 kHz) are out of reach inside a 180 Hz PhysX step ([cosin/VI-grade](https://www.cosin.eu/wp-content/uploads/VI-grade_2018.pdf); [BeamNG.tech architecture](https://documentation.beamng.com/beamng_tech/architecture/)).

The missing `eNONE` decides how an enveloping model can reach PhysX in Isaac. A live cam result cannot replace the query plane, so it has two uses. The first is offline: run the cam over the road once and cook the result as a separate, pre-filtered "effective road" surface that only the wheel queries see, in the ground-query group, while the true geometry renders and is seen by lidar. That costs nothing at run time and reproduces the early rise, the enveloping and the effective slope. It cannot depend on speed or capture impact dynamics, and NVIDIA documents nothing like it, so it must be tested. The second is live: run the cam in the per-step hook as a measurement and as the input to the corner-force corrections described below. The PhysX wheel also has **no tire vertical stiffness attribute** (checked in the Isaac 6.1 install), while a real passenger tire has roughly 200-250 kN/m, for example 236.7 kN/m in one published `.tir` file ([Eng-Tips](https://eng-tips.com/threads/pacejka-coefficents-for-lateral-dynamics.348016)). Rim strikes and pinch flats therefore cannot emerge on their own. A sidewall-deflection check computed from the effective height can still flag them as events.

## PhysX's massless corner is what makes rocks launch and washboard grip

The suspension behind that query is equally thin. vehicle2's force parameters are stiffness, damping and sprung mass, with one linear, symmetric damper and no bump and rebound split ([PxVehicleSuspensionForceParams](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.6.1/_api_build/structPxVehicleSuspensionForceParams.html)). The wheel's mass appears only in its spin dynamics. Nothing in the suspension or wheel parameters gives the wheel a vertical degree of freedom ([PhysX 5.4.1 Vehicles guide](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.4.1/docs/Vehicles.html)). No NVIDIA page says outright that the wheel has no vertical state. That conclusion is inferred from the documented "place the wheel on the ground" rule. Its consequences are concrete, though. **A snapped wheel never has to be accelerated down into a pothole, so it never unloads, and there is no 10-15 Hz wheel-hop mode** to resonate on washboard. Full compression is not a bump stop. The wheel is clamped and a constraint "pushes the vehicle upward" over the next step, and a progressive bump-stop spring is explicitly left to the user ([PhysX 5.4.1 Vehicles guide](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.4.1/docs/Vehicles.html)). The SDK's limit constraint takes a restitution and a direction, and the guide recommends `eROAD_GEOMETRY_NORMAL` to avoid self-righting ([PxVehiclePhysXSuspensionLimitConstraintParams](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.5.0/_api_build/structPxVehiclePhysXSuspensionLimitConstraintParams.html)). Isaac 6.1 exposes neither, and it has no anti-roll bar API either, although the SDK has `PxVehicleAntiRollBar` (checked in the Isaac 6.1 install). Isaac does expose toe and camber compliance graphs and force-application points, which aib leaves constant (aib source).

The spec puts this car close to the rigid stop. aib tunes each axle so its static sag is g/ω²: **about 0.10 m of the 0.2 m travel at 1.6 Hz**, which leaves **about 0.10 m of bump travel and 0.10 m of droop** (own arithmetic from aib's tuning; all three spec values are marked "assumed"). Any rock or edge much over 10 cm therefore goes straight into the rigid limit constraint, and the chassis takes the whole impulse in about one 5.6 ms step. A real elastomer or hydraulic stop spreads it over 10-30 ms, so peaks will be roughly 2-5 times too high and too short (inference from the step size). Rally cars run **250-300 mm or more of travel** with hydraulic bump stops tuned for landings, and stiffer fast-compression damping on jump-heavy stages ([engineerfix](https://engineerfix.com/what-is-a-rally-car-engineering-safety-and-classes/); [Škoda Storyboard](https://www.skoda-storyboard.com/en/lifestyle/motorsport/how-to-fly-a-rally-car/)). A landing from a 0.5 m crest arrives at √(2gh) = 3.1 m/s. For a 1760 kg car that is about 8.5 kJ of vertical energy, which over 0.15 m of stroke is an average of about 57 kN, roughly 3.3 g. That is a sizing target for a bump stop, not a measurement (own arithmetic).

The physics rate matters for the same reason. A correctly integrated 12-13 Hz unsprung mode would be fine at 180 Hz, but PhysX does not have one. Simulators that do model unsprung dynamics run at **333-2000 Hz**: rFactor 2 at about 400 Hz, RBR at 720 Hz since the NGP 7.0 update, BeamNG at 2000 Hz (community figures; [overtake.gg](https://www.overtake.gg/threads/what-rate-do-physics-run-at-1000hz-500.159380/); [io-tech forum](https://bbs.io-tech.fi/threads/ajopeliketju.27082/post-11836285); [BeamNG.tech](https://documentation.beamng.com/beamng_tech/architecture/)). aib's eight vehicle substeps put the spring and tire update at about 1440 Hz, but the limit constraint and every chassis contact go through the scene's rigid-body solve at 180 Hz. Whether omni.physx re-substeps the limit constraint is unverified.

Two things already in Isaac 6.1 make the gaps fillable from outside. The first is the schema's `physxVehicle:limitSuspensionExpansionVelocity` (default false). It limits how fast the spring can extend, so the wheel can only fall as fast as the spring pushes it. NVIDIA warns that "the wheel might loose contact with the ground more easily" and then "the tire cannot deliver longitudinal or lateral force" (checked in the Isaac 6.1 install; [PhysX 5.5.1 Vehicles guide](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.5.1/docs/Vehicles.html)). For potholes and washboard that loss of contact is the point. It is a crude stand-in for unsprung inertia and the only built-in way a wheel ever unloads. The second is Isaac's per-wheel state, which reports **suspension jounce, suspension force, ground plane, hit position and the ground actor** (`VEHICLE_WHEEL_STATE_SUSPENSION_JOUNCE`, `_GROUND_ACTOR` and others, checked in the Isaac 6.1 install). aib already reads part of it every step and applies a force through `PhysxForceAPI` for the road loads (aib source). That is the hook a corner-force layer needs. Each step it reads jounce and jounce speed, computes a progressive bump-stop force, the difference between a digressive asymmetric damper and PhysX's linear one, and the anti-roll torque, and applies them to the chassis at the suspension attachment as a force plus its equivalent torque. The PhysX travel is set slightly longer than the real travel, so the rigid limit becomes a last resort behind the modelled stop. One limitation remains. The tire's grip comes from PhysX's own spring force, so the added forces reach the tire load only if they are also folded into the per-wheel friction or stiffness aib already writes (`set_wheel_friction`, `set_wheel_stiffness`).

Beyond that layer, fidelity costs weeks. A virtual unsprung mass per corner (about 40-45 kg, not the spec's 20 kg wheel, which is right only for spin inertia), integrated at about 1 kHz against a tire spring, would give real wheel hop and washboard grip loss. Its explicit integration at 180 Hz is stable but loses 5-10 % of amplitude per cycle (inference). To avoid double-counting, PhysX's own spring would have to be nulled, which fights the PhysX tire's dependence on suspension force for normal load. An articulated Isaac vehicle with four wheel bodies on prismatic joints would lose the PhysX tire model, ESC and everything aib has calibrated. Chrono::Vehicle co-simulation, with full double-wishbone templates ([Chrono Sedan_DoubleWishbone](https://api.chrono.projectchrono.org/classchrono_1_1vehicle_1_1sedan_1_1_sedan___double_wishbone.html)), would make Isaac a renderer and Chrono the physics owner. Neither is worth it until the cheaper stages are measured and found wanting.

## One height function gives physics, cameras and lidar the same road

ISO 8608 fits road roughness as Gd(n) = Gd(n0)·(n/n0)^−w with n0 = 0.1 cycles/m and w = 2, in classes A-H a factor of 4 apart. The geometric means run from 16 to 262 144 × 10⁻⁶ m³, and it generates profiles by summing cosines with random phase, amplitude √(2·Δn·Gd(n)) ([Agostinacchio et al. 2014](https://d-nb.info/1100862730/34)). The standard sets the lower frequency limit at **0.05 cycles/m for off-road vehicles** and the upper at **10 cycles/m**, because tyre enveloping already filters wavelengths shorter than 0.1 m ([ISO 8608:2016 preview](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/71202/05b2151f255b44928f80acb897fc0c2c/ISO-8608-2016.pdf)). Over that band, w = 2 gives RMS heights of about 14 mm for class D, 29 mm for E and 57 mm for F (own computation). Maintained gravel behaves like C-E, and F and above are rough tracks. Gravel does not follow a single w = 2 line. A measured Lithuanian gravel road shows **a spectral break at 2 cycles/m (0.5 m) in every state and a second at about 6.5 cycles/m** in its worst state, both attributed to corrugation. Its large loose grains did not affect the vehicle because the tyre enveloped them ([Žuraulis et al. 2021](https://d-nb.info/1257077627/34)). The same paper found IRI misleading on gravel: the visibly worst road had the lowest IRI. So a synthetic road should be checked on its spectral shape, not on IRI alone. The left and right wheel tracks are only partly coherent, and that coherence varies along the road. Bogsjö's one-parameter exponential model is the practical choice, with roughness and coherence drawn per segment from Weibull distributions. That data is from asphalt only ([Lamb & Rouillard 2021](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/42925/1/2021Synthesizing_dual-track_road_elevation_data.pdf)). Since the car can be anywhere across a 3-6 m road, the base layer should be a 2D field with slightly longer lateral correlation, rather than two tracks.

The discrete features have documented geometry and documented places. Washboard is a speed-threshold instability of loose, low-plasticity gravel. Its onset is near 1.5 m/s and its ripples drift slowly forward ([Taberlet et al., PRL 2007](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0711.3872)). The field rule is **wavelength in cm ≈ the modal traffic speed in km/h**, it seldom forms in the wet season, and it rarely forms on roads with a modal speed under 20 km/h, which covers tight forestry hairpins ([TRH 20, 2009](https://www.transport.gov.za/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/draftTRH20.15x.pdf)). It forms "on hills, on curves, in areas of acceleration or deceleration". Severity is under 2.5 cm, 2.5-7.5 cm, or over 7.5 cm deep, and the same manual bins potholes (1.5-10+ cm deep, under 0.3 m to over 1 m across), ruts and berms (under 5, 5-10, over 10 cm) ([USACE TM 5-626](https://www.wbdg.org/FFC/ARMYCOE/COETM/tm_5_626.pdf)). Potholes concentrate at sags, on flat poorly cambered sections and near culverts and bridges, and recur at the same place for years ([TRH 20](https://www.transport.gov.za/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/draftTRH20.15x.pdf)). The FHWA recommends a **4 % crown, never over 6 %**, a straight "A" shape rather than a parabola, foreslopes of 1V:3H (never steeper than 1V:2H) and flat-bottom ditches ([FHWA Gravel Roads Guide 2015](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/construction/pubs/ots15002.pdf)). Japanese forest roads (林業専用道) differ in ways that matter for a Japanese stage. The design speed is 15 km/h, the carriageway 3.0 m, there is **no superelevation and a level cross-section**, and water leaves through rolling grades and cross drains instead of a crown ([高知県林業専用道作設指針](https://www.pref.kochi.lg.jp/doc/senyoudou/file_contents/file_20215182151026_1.pdf)). One Tokyo survey found 92 % of cross drains were rubber water bars ([東京都農林水産振興財団](https://www.tokyo-aff.or.jp/uploaded/attachment/7259.pdf)). Their height is not sourced, and 幹線林道 built to older standards may differ.

The generation pipeline follows directly. Build a road-aligned (u, v) frame on the centreline, OpenCRG-style. Shape the cross-section as a function of v: crown or level, shoulder break, foreslope, ditch and backslope, overriding a 5-10 m DEM that cannot hold any of it. Add the 2D broadband field with a gravel-shaped piecewise spectrum, modulated per 50-200 m segment. Then add overlays placed by rule. Washboard gets an asymmetric sawtooth at λ ≈ v_modal/100, weighted to braking zones, corner exits and climbs. Ruts are two Gaussian bands at half the track width with a gravel windrow between them. Potholes are Poisson-placed with a sag weighting. Water bars are oblique at 30-45° with spacing set by grade, using USFS- or TRH-style tables ([Roads for Water](https://roadsforwater.org/guideline/water-harvesting-and-drainage-from-unpaved-roads/use-basic-road-surface-drainage/)). Rally-stage berms and the edge windrow go at the shoulder. Seed everything per tile so a tile that streams back in is identical. Check the result by computing its PSD and a golden-quarter-car IRI.

PhysX heightfields are the right container for the corridor. A sample is a 16-bit height plus two 7-bit material indices, 4 bytes in all. Holes, boundary-edge suppression for tiling, and `modifySamples()` for runtime edits are supported ([PhysX 5.5.1 Geometry](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.5.1/docs/Geometry.html)). At **5 cm spacing a 20 m corridor costs about 32 MB per km**. A triangle mesh of the same grid is roughly 9 times larger (own arithmetic), so a 10 km stage needs streaming tiles, not one resident field. Five centimetres puts six or more samples on a 0.3 m washboard wave and three or four across the contact patch, which is everything the 10 cycles/m enveloping limit lets a tire feel. Sharp edges such as pothole lips and rocks belong in meshes. Two cautions apply. Quantization is range/32767, so a tile spanning a whole mountain (1000 m of relief) quantizes heights to 3 cm, which flattens washboard. The corridor tiles need their own height range. And heightfields are axis-aligned, so a winding road needs tiles rotated to the local heading or world-aligned tiles over the corridor's bounding boxes. Whether Isaac 6.1 runs heightfields on the GPU pipeline was not found in the docs.

Consistency is a rendering constraint as much as a physics one. RTX real-time support for material displacement is limited: OmniSurface displacement is "not supported yet", and MDL displacement is off by default ([Omniverse materials](https://docs.omniverse.nvidia.com/app_view/prod_materials-and-rendering/materials.html); [RTX common settings](https://docs.omniverse.nvidia.com/materials-and-rendering/latest/rtx-renderer_common.html)). RTX lidar traces render geometry. So every bump the wheels feel and the lidar should see must be real render geometry sampled from the same function, and normal maps should carry only sub-centimetre texture for the camera. rFactor-style per-surface contact noise, a 10 mm `RoadBumpAmp` ([SimWiki](https://simwiki.net/wiki/Remove_Unwanted_Car_and_FFB_Bumps_and_Bounces_in_GTR2)), is cheap but invisible to sensors, so it is acceptable only below about 5 mm. The localization map should be built from simulated lidar over this geometry, not from the generator, so the map contains exactly what the sensors return. Exporting the corridor as ASAM OpenCRG ([ASAM OpenCRG format](https://asam-ev.github.io/OpenCRG/asamopencrg/latest/specification/06_opencrg_data_format/6_0_Open_crg_data_format.html)) is optional, but it gives a standard file other vehicle-dynamics tools can drive for cross-checks.

## Rocks split into three classes, and only a few hundred should ever move

No game or simulator publishes a rock-size threshold, but every documented system keeps most rocks static. A BeamNG programmer said the team "intentionally reduced the amount of dynamic objects in official stages" for performance ([BeamNG forum](https://beamng.com/posts/1866850)). Dirt Rally's thrown stones are particles and audio ([Wikipedia: Dirt Rally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirt_Rally); [Overtake: Particles+](https://www.overtake.gg/downloads/particles.8651/)). Rover twins treat rocks as rigid obstacles that wheels "slipped around" when too large ([POLAR-Sim](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.12397)). Geometry gives the split for this car (own inference, to be tuned by test). A stone under about 0.1 R (4 cm) is surface, handled by the existing gravel friction and particle effects. Above about 0.3 R it matters to the wheel. For the 0.370 m wheel a 10 cm stone already meets the tire at a 43° contact angle, and a stone as tall as R is a wall. Above the ground clearance it must also hit the underbody. **Static boulders** (a 0.6 m basalt block weighs about 300 kg) should be drivable and should also collide with the chassis. **Dynamic rocks of roughly 1-50 kg (9-33 cm)** are the band a car should visibly shove, roll or launch. A placement model is available from planetary work. The cumulative area fraction of rocks of diameter at least D is F(D) = k·exp(−q(k)·D), with q = 1.79 + 0.152/k ([Golombek et al. 2021](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/d1t32-akr32/files/Earth_and_Space_Science_-_2021_-_Golombek_-_Rock_Size_u2010Frequency_Distributions_at_the_InSight_Landing_Site_Mars.pdf?download=1); [NTRS 20030111177](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20030111177)). Run it with a lateral k profile, low in the wheel paths and high on shoulders, verges and water-cut ruts, and place rocks by Poisson-disk sampling. Statistics from real gravel roads were not found.

The PhysX settings are well documented. Dynamic rocks should be convex hulls of **at most 64 vertices and polygons**, or GPU contact generation falls back to the CPU ([PhysX 5.4.0 GPU Rigid Bodies](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.4.0/docs/GPURigidBodies.html)). Avoid SDF colliders, which one user measured at about 20 times slower than convex hulls ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/sdf-mesh-collision-performance/270825)). Use **speculative CCD**, because sweep CCD is processed on the CPU even in a GPU scene. Tunnelling is real here: at 30 m/s a body moves 0.167 m per 180 Hz step, more than a 1-10 kg stone's size ([PhysX 5.4.1 Advanced Collision Detection](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.4.1/docs/AdvancedCollisionDetection.html)). Use TGS for the 360:1 mass ratio between the car and a 5 kg stone, or a 1:0 dominance group so small stones cannot push the car ([PhysX 5.4.1 Rigid Body Dynamics](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.4.1/docs/RigidBodyDynamics.html)). The default GPU buffers suit about 10 000 bodies, and overflow silently drops contacts ([PhysX GPU Rigid Bodies](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.4.0/docs/GPURigidBodies.html)). Spawn the dynamic class only within a radius of each vehicle, about 30 m/s × 2 s + 20 m ≈ 80 m. Create the rocks asleep and write their moved poses back to the tile record when they leave, the pattern Township Tale used so that streaming never woke everything ([Township Tale](https://townshiptale.com/news/streaming-a-physical-world)). Then only 10²-10³ rocks are ever awake. Full DEM gravel is out of the question: 10 million grains for 3 s took up to 7 hours on two A100s ([Chrono::CRM, arXiv 2507.05643](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.05643v2)).

The hard question is how a rock meets a PhysX wheel, because the wheel is a query and not a body. Whatever is in the ground-query group becomes the road plane, and "non-drivable surfaces interact with wheels only through rigid body contact" ([PhysX Vehicles manual](https://archive.docs.nvidia.com/gameworks/content/gameworkslibrary/physx/guide/Manual/Vehicles.html)). aib has no wheel shapes, so a non-drivable rock would pass through the wheels and meet only the chassis. Making rocks drivable lets the tire climb them through the suspension, which is the right path for the jump the brief asks about. The opposite force on the rock is apparently not applied: no source says the SDK pushes back on a dynamic hit actor. A drivable stone is then an immovable step that never rolls. The fix is to read the ground actor from the wheel state and apply −(suspension force) at the hit point to that rock ourselves, a pattern one developer reports "works well enough" ([gamedev.net](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/685988-raycast-vehicle-physics-problem)). The other choice, rigid wheel shapes that collide with rocks, sends the impact straight into the sprung mass with no suspension or tire compliance, and NVIDIA's own factory filters wheel shapes away from ground (local `omni.physx.vehicle` `Factory.py`). Use it only for stones too small to drive on. The underbody needs its own fix. aib's chassis box bottom sits at r + 0.05 m, **about 0.42 m with the spec's 0.370 m wheel and roughly three times a sedan's clearance**, so no rock strikes it today (aib source). A thin skid-plate collider at the real clearance, in a group that collides with rocks and terrain, gives strikes and high-centering through ordinary rigid contact. That contact is rigid and impulsive. `maxDepenetrationVelocity` and a small contact offset govern how violent it is ([Omni Physics collision guide](https://docs.omniverse.nvidia.com/kit/docs/omni_physics/latest/dev_guide/guides/collision_guide.html)). NPC cars that SSv2 moves by `set_transform` are kinematic-like and will crush stones they cross, so filter them out of the rock group.

## The open record supports shape checks, not force matching

Validation can rest on tests with clear definitions, even though their reference numbers are mostly paywalled. SAE J2731 rolls a tire over perpendicular and inclined cleats at very low speed and records spindle force against distance ([SAE J2731](https://saemobilus.sae.org/content/j2731_201805)). SAE J2730 is its dynamic counterpart ([SAE J2730](https://saemobilus.sae.org/content/J2730_200608/)). Zegelaar's 0.2 km/h drum data are the basis of the cam models ([Pacejka ch. 10](https://www.scribd.com/document/535583154/70165-10)). The cleat check that needs no data is geometric: vertical force should start rising about 0.25 m before the hub reaches a 10 cm step, the longitudinal force should kick backwards, and the effective rolling radius should dip. Raycast fails all three. A pothole has a published *shape*. Michigan Scientific's SUV at 40 km/h shows the vertical load falling as the wheel drops in from a static value just under 6.7 kN, then the largest peak at the far-edge strike, with a longitudinal spike, all **within 0.5 s** ([Michigan Scientific](https://www.michsci-europe.com/durability-campaigns)). Edge-strike magnitudes are tens of kN: **37.7 kN** for a Golf hitting a convex block at 64 km/h ([US 10386270](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10386270)), and wheel force transducers are ranged to 40-50 kN ([MTS SWIFT Evo](https://www.mts.com/en/products/test-system-components/swift-evo)). A trace with no load drop means the wheel is snapping. A peak of only a few kN means the edge is being filtered away.

Ride quality has standard yardsticks. ISO 2631-1 bands frequency-weighted RMS from under 0.315 m/s² (not uncomfortable) through 0.8-1.6 (uncomfortable) to over 2 (extremely uncomfortable) ([Engineering Toolbox](https://www.engineeringtoolbox.com/people-vibration-d_1292.html)). It switches to the vibration dose value above a crest factor of 9, which is the regime of rocks, potholes and landings ([ride-comfort summary](https://www.scribd.com/document/385036592/ride-comfort-pdf)). IRI comes from the golden quarter-car (normalized c_s = 6, k_t = 653, k_s = 63.3, m_u/m_s = 0.15). Granlund puts ISO-weighted floor vibration at about **0.16 m/s² per mm/m of IRI at 80 km/h** ([Granlund](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/iri-vs-iso2631/34121158)). Washboard at 0.5-1 m pitch ([Taberlet](https://perso.ens-lyon.fr/nicolas.taberlet/washboard/)), driven at 30-60 km/h, excites 8-33 Hz, on and above the wheel-hop band. That makes it the test that exposes the massless corner. For datasets, only **TartanDrive** records shock travel and wheel speed (a Yamaha Viking ATV; rear shocks only in 2.0, because the front sensors kept breaking) ([TartanDrive](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.01791); [TartanDrive 2.0](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01913)). RELLIS-3D (100 Hz IMU) and Boreas (200 Hz Applanix) support acceleration spectra ([RELLIS-3D](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.12954); [Boreas](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.10168)). None is a road car on gravel, so comparisons can only be about trends: PSD peaks at 1-2 Hz (body) and 10-15 Hz (wheel hop), and how RMS scales with speed and roughness. Absolute levels cannot be matched.

## Roughness reaches Autoware through pitch, ground filtering and the map

The sensor consequences are the reason this matters for an autonomy testbed. Pitch dominates the lidar error. A DARPA team found "small amounts of vibration ... especially in the pitch axis, result in large range errors, as the scan hits the ground in the wrong place", which produced false obstacles and cut their usable range roughly in half ([Overbot Note #51](http://www.overbot.com/grandchallenge/note51.html)). For a roof lidar about 1.9 m up, 0.5° of pitch makes the ground at 20 m appear about 17 cm too high or too low (own arithmetic). That is above typical non-ground height thresholds. Autoware's `scan_ground_filter` documents the trade-off this forces: one threshold set "misclassifies uneven ground as obstacles", and the other "incorrectly classifies small obstacles, such as rocks, as ground" ([Autoware in Construction, arXiv 2610.04187](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.04187); [scan_ground_filter docs](https://autowarefoundation.github.io/autoware.universe/pr-8870/perception/autoware_ground_segmentation/docs/scan-ground-filter)). Unfiltered ground points are a confirmed cause of obstacle stops on a clear road ([Autoware discussion #7038](https://github.com/orgs/autowarefoundation/discussions/7038)). Negative obstacles such as potholes and ditches lose lidar information "much more rapidly" with range than positive ones ([Lebakula 2021](https://scholarsjunction.msstate.edu/td/5329)). A 0.3 m rock at 50 m gets only a couple of rings (own arithmetic). The road features are therefore real perception test cases, and they must exist in render geometry to be tested at all.

Isaac's lidar model needs two settings changed before it reproduces any of this. **Motion BVH is off by default**, and without it RTX lidar motion compensation does not work correctly. Turning it on "can significantly increase rendering time" ([Isaac Sim 6.1 RTX Sensors](https://docs.isaacsim.omniverse.nvidia.com/6.1.0/sensors/isaacsim_sensors_rtx.html)). Without it, washboard and wheel-hop vibration at 10-30 Hz will not smear a scan the way it does on a real car. Multi-echo `echoId` is always 0, so the strongest-versus-last-return behaviour that dominates real dust handling cannot be reproduced ([same](https://docs.isaacsim.omniverse.nvidia.com/6.1.0/sensors/isaacsim_sensors_rtx.html)). Dust is better left out than modelled halfway. Sensors are rigidly attached, so they inherit chassis motion exactly. Real mounts add compliance (Overbot measured "a few degrees" from mount and frame flex), so a small mount-jitter model belongs with any rough-road test. On forest roads, NDT faces both a corridor of tree trunks, which is self-similar along the road ([Tuna et al., arXiv 2408.11809](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.11809v1)), and pitch and roll excitation of its initial guess. That links this work to the project's NDT step-reversal fix (docs/issues/002) and to issue 017, NDT lost at 30 m/s.

R0 and R1 come first because they are almost free and turn every later argument into a measurement. The fixtures will show at once how bad the snap and the pop are. They will also show whether the sweep's artefacts outweigh its timing gain and whether the expansion limit unsettles the ECU's ABS, TC and ESC, which were tuned on a car whose wheels never unload. R2 is the largest early investment and the one everything else stands on. Without a corridor of real geometry there is nothing for rocks to sit on, nothing for the corner layer to absorb, and nothing for Autoware to misread. It also has the cleanest acceptance tests, because the generator's output can be checked against its own target spectrum before any car drives on it. R3 is where the brief's "a car that hits a rock may jump" becomes trustworthy. R1 makes the jump happen at the right moment, and R3 makes it the right size, by replacing a one-step rigid impulse and a linear damper with a stop and a damper that behave like hardware. R4 can run in parallel with R3 once R2 exists. It is mostly configuration and pooling code, and its largest unknown, whether PhysX already pushes back on a dynamic hit actor, can be settled in an afternoon on the fixture course. R5 and R6 refine what the earlier stages already produce. R7 is held back deliberately. It is the only stage that fights the PhysX vehicle rather than working around it, and it should be built only if the washboard fixture shows that expansion-limited lift-off is not enough.

Several questions should be settled before or during R0 and R1, since each one changes a later stage. Does the PhysX vehicle query once per scene step or once per vehicle substep? That decides whether the damper spike is about 36 kN or several times larger. Does omni.physx re-substep the suspension-limit constraint? Does Isaac 6.1 run PhysX heightfields on the GPU, and how many tiles can be resident? Can the ground-query group really hold different geometry from the rendered road without side effects? And what does a sweep cost per wheel with eight vehicles in the scene?

## Conclusion

The brief asks for a car that jumps when it hits a rock. In this stack the difficulty is that it jumps for the wrong reason. A raycast wheel on a massless corner with a rigid stop already launches over a step, but late, through a damper spike tens of times larger than real hardware allows, and with an impulse one step long. So realism here is mostly a matter of *removing* false violence, then supplying the compliance PhysX leaves out. That reframes the work. The first deliverable is not rocks at all. It is the instrumented fixture course and the corner-force layer, measured against the shapes the literature does publish: a cleat force that starts a quarter of a metre early, a pothole load that drops before it spikes, a landing spread over tens of milliseconds.

The second insight is that road geometry is shared infrastructure, not a vehicle feature. Generated once and sampled three ways, the same corridor serves the wheels, the cameras and the lidar map. Its features (washboard at braking zones, potholes in sags, rocks on the shoulders, level 林道 sections with water bars) are exactly the cases where Autoware's ground filter, distortion correction and NDT are least proven. A stage that drives believably over that corridor is also, at no extra cost, the most demanding perception test the project can run. That makes R2 and R6 worth doing even if the vehicle never gets a wheel-hop mode.
